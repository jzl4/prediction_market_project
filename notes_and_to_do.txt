# Background about the data & sports betting markets
- The vendor is River Markets, which is a prime broker / unified execution layer for prediction markets - one API across venues, live on Kalshi, Polymarket, Polymarket.  They raised $8.5 million seed announced on Aug 11th, 2026
- The ID native_id, such as KXMLBTOTAL-26AUG051940PITMIL-9, is one specific bet, such as "Will the Fed hike in September" or "Will the Giants win the Superbowl?".  One question, one order book, one price.  Prices are between 0 and 1, and correspond to an implied probability of the event happening. For instance, a price of 0.47 means the market believes there is a 47% chance that Fed will hike in Sept. If someone buys at 0.47, and the Fed does hike, he receives $1 and makes a profit of $0.43
- Distinction between native_id vs. river_id: river_id is River's venue-neutral number for the same thing, so their customers can manage one set of identifiers, instead of dealing with both Kalshi's identifiers and Polymarket's condition-ID identifiers. The reason why they're 1-to-1 here is because this file quotes markets/bets from only one venue.  If instead, the bet/prediction traded on both Kalshi and Polymarket, we'd get 2 different river_ids.  For the purposes of this exercise, just treat native_id and river_id as the same

# Baseball
- The prediction markets in this dataset relate to baseball, specifically concerning the number of runs (a player crossing home plate)
- Example: P(runs >= 8) = 0.445 means 44.5% chance of there being 8 or more runs in this game
- Therefore, the strike k where the probability crosses 0.5 (50% chance) is where the market expects the median number of runs to happen in a game. It's around k = 7 to 8 runs, because P(runs >= 7) and P(runs >= 8) are the closest values to 50%. Specifically, this happens at strike 7 with probability 0.565 and strike 8 with probability 0.445.  But this is the median, not the average/mean.  The mean is higher because the run distribution is necessarily bounded at zero on the left side and unbounded on the right side (number of runs must be 0 or greater, but there is no upper limit), so mean > median due to right-skew
- The reason why the strikes are at half integer increments like 1.5, is because the event can never expire on that strike.  It must expire at 1, 2, 3, etc, so it is unambiguous whether or not the contract pays out or not.  For instance, contract KXMLBTOTAL-9 means 9 or more runs, which is identical to "over 8.5".  In other words, strike of 1.5 means P(number or runs is 2 or more).  So P(number of runs = exactly 2) = P(runs >= 2) - P(runs >= 3)
- team_totals is one team on its own; totals is both teams combined. The data:

line                       1.5    2.5    3.5    4.5    5.5    6.5    7.5
Milwaukee Brewers;Over    0.805  0.650  0.495  0.355  0.235  0.155  0.095
Pittsburgh Pirates;Over   0.770  0.605  0.450  0.315  0.210  0.135  0.085

game TOTAL at line 3.5  = 0.845

At the same 3.5 strike, each individual team is near 0.45-0.50 but the combined game total is 0.845 — because getting 4+ runs is far easier for two teams together than for either alone. Each team's median is ~3.5 runs; the two sum to ~7, which lines up with the game total's median of 7-8. That's your additivity constraint holding.

That's also why there are 14 team-total markets: 2 teams × 7 strikes. And it's visible in outcome_name, which reads "Milwaukee Brewers;Over" vs "Pittsburgh Pirates;Over" — the team is named because the contract is about that team alone. Incidentally, Milwaukee prices above Pittsburgh at every strike, so the market expects Milwaukee to score more.

Every contract in this file is cumulative — P(runs ≥ n). That's a survival function, and it is monotonically decreasing by definition. That's your call-spread argument and it always holds:

P(>= 2) = 0.965
P(>= 3) = 0.935
P(>= 4) = 0.845   ... strictly decreasing all the way down to

P(>=12) = 0.145

Note: This is actually 1 - CDF from statistics, because in statistics, we quote P(X < some constant k), which is the other way around.  In sports betting on Kalshi, this is the survival function, which is the complement of CDF.  Survival function must be monotonically decreasing, just like how the CDF is monotonically increasing towards 1.0

But P(exactly n) is what you get by differencing adjacent strikes, and that is emphatically not monotone — it's a hump:

P(exactly  1) = 0.035  ####
P(exactly  2) = 0.030  ###
P(exactly  3) = 0.090  ##########
P(exactly  5) = 0.120  ##############
P(exactly  7) = 0.120  ##############
P(exactly  9) = 0.100  ###########
P(exactly 11) = 0.070  ########

P(exactly 7 runs) is nearly four times P(exactly 1 run). Runs are roughly Poisson-distributed with a mean near 8, so the density peaks in the middle and falls off both sides.

Note: This is basically the PMF (probability mass function from university statistics), and it is the derivative of the survival or CDF function

Me: totals vs team_totals is X + Y = Z, so it's a convolution. In baseball, what is the
shape of the distributions for X and Y closest to? If X ~ Poisson(l1) and Y ~ Poisson(l2)
then Z ~ Poisson(l1+l2). If binomial with same p, Z ~ Binomial(n1+n2, p). But this assumes

The convolution logic is exactly right, including both closure results (Poisson closure
holds generally; the binomial one requires the SAME p, which is why n1+n2 works - it's just
pooling Bernoulli trials).

The answer to "what shape" is: NOT Poisson, and not binomial - NEGATIVE BINOMIAL.
And our own ladders prove it.

The three classic discrete distributions are distinguished by variance-to-mean ratio:

    Binomial             var/mean < 1     underdispersed
    Poisson              var/mean = 1     the reference case
    Negative binomial    var/mean > 1     overdispersed

Fitting each market-implied ladder by least squares on the survival points:

    MIL (team)   Poisson: lam=3.75              RMSE=0.0599
                 NegBin : mean=3.89  var=7.29   var/mean=1.87   RMSE=0.0048  (12x better)

    PIT (team)   Poisson: lam=3.48              RMSE=0.0643
                 NegBin : mean=3.63  var=7.18   var/mean=1.98   RMSE=0.0033  (19x better)

    GAME TOTAL   Poisson: lam=7.29              RMSE=0.0666
                 NegBin : mean=7.52  var=15.89  var/mean=2.11   RMSE=0.0092  (7x better)

    FIRST 5      Poisson: lam=3.80              RMSE=0.0668
                 NegBin : mean=4.04  var=8.62   var/mean=2.13   RMSE=0.0078  (9x better)

Every ladder lands at var/mean ~ 1.9-2.1. Baseball runs have roughly TWICE the variance a
Poisson would allow. The negative binomial fits to RMSE 0.003-0.009 - under one cent, which
is inside the bid-ask spread, so it's fitting as well as the data can resolve.

This matches the sabermetrics literature: runs per team per game are a standard example of
overdispersion, and the negative binomial (Tom Tango's "Enby" distribution is a refinement)
is the accepted workhorse. Steven Miller's Weibull model is the other well-known choice,
used to derive the Pythagorean win expectation.

WHY BASEBALL BREAKS POISSON: a Poisson process needs independent increments - each scoring
opportunity independent of the last. Baseball violates this structurally. Runs don't arrive
one at a time at a constant rate; they arrive in INNINGS, IN BURSTS. A rally puts runners on
base, which raises the probability of the next run, and a home run with two men on scores
three at once. Three outs then shut the inning down entirely. So you get many innings with
zero and occasional innings with four - clustering, which is exactly overdispersion. The
standard formalization is a Poisson-gamma mixture (lambda itself random, varying by matchup,
weather, bullpen), and that mixture IS the negative binomial.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE CONVOLUTION TEST - THIS IS THE PAYOFF
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Fit each team separately, convolve, compare to the market's independently-quoted game total:

           market S(n)   Poisson conv     NegBin conv
    n= 4        0.845      0.930 (+.085)   0.867 (+.022)
    n= 7        0.565      0.584 (+.019)   0.560 (-.005)
    n= 9        0.365      0.302 (-.063)   0.354 (-.011)
    n=12        0.145      0.065 (-.080)   0.146 (+.001)

    mean |error|             0.0601          0.0104
    implied E[Z]             7.23            7.52

The negative binomial convolution reproduces the ENTIRE game-total ladder to about ONE CENT
- inside the spread, so it's consistent with the market to the limit of measurement. Poisson
is six times worse and wrong SYSTEMATICALLY: too fat in the middle, far too thin in the tail.
At n=12 it says 0.065 where the market says 0.145 - less than half. That tail error is
precisely the overdispersion being thrown away.

TWO CONSTRAINTS COME OUT CLEAN:

(a) MEANS ADD EXACTLY.  MIL 3.89 + PIT 3.63 = 7.52, and the game total fitted independently
    gives 7.52. Additivity holding to two decimals across separately-quoted markets.

(b) WE CAN BACK OUT THE CORRELATION.  Since Var(X+Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2*Cov(X,Y):

        7.29 + 7.18 = 14.47   (what independence would give)
                      15.89   (what the game-total ladder implies)

    The gap implies Cov ~ 0.71, so RHO ~ +0.10. The market is pricing a small POSITIVE
    correlation between the two teams' scores - which makes baseball sense: both teams share
    the ballpark, the weather and wind, the umpire's strike zone, and the general run
    environment of the night. Slugfests and pitchers' duels both tend to be mutual.

    So independence isn't exact, but now we have a number for how wrong it is: small enough
    that the first-order convolution is a genuinely good approximation, which is why the
    NegBin convolution came within a cent.

CAVEATS BEFORE LEANING ON THIS: the team ladders only go to strike 8 while the game total
runs to 12, so the team tail variances are EXTRAPOLATED rather than observed - the rho ~ 0.10
estimate is the softest number here. And all of it is fitted to MID prices, which carry half
a spread of noise. Fitting to bid and ask separately would give a band rather than a point
estimate.

REFINEMENT 1: it's <=, NOT strictly <.
F5 runs EQUAL total runs whenever nobody scores in innings 6-9, which is a perfectly
ordinary game. So as random variables:

        RFI  <=  F5  <=  TOTAL

and that holds PATHWISE - on every single realization, not just on average. That's the
strong form. Inning 1 is contained in innings 1-5, which is contained in innings 1-9. We're
not comparing two distributions that happen to be ordered; we're comparing nested sums of
the same non-negative quantities.

Pathwise containment is what GIVES us the survival-function inequality (this is first-order
stochastic dominance):

        S_RFI(n)  <=  S_F5(n)  <=  S_TOTAL(n)     for every n

REFINEMENT 2: "for all strikes" is only ONE strike for RFI.
RFI is a single binary contract at line 0.5 - there's no ladder, because "at least 1 run" is
the only question it asks. So the RFI-vs-F5 comparison is testable at exactly one point, n=1.

So the constraint set for the no-arb check is:

1. Within a chain — S(n) monotonically decreasing, and every differenced bucket ≥ 0 (call spreads non-negative, butterflies non-negative).
2. Across nested chains — RFI ⊆ F5 ⊆ game total, so at any shared strike, S_RFI(n) ≤ S_F5(n) ≤ S_total(n).
3. Teams to total — the convolution of the two team distributions should reproduce the game-total distribution; the weak but always-valid version is S_total(n) ≥ max(S_MIL(n), S_PIT(n)).
4. Means add exactly — E[MIL] + E[PIT] = E[total].

Constraint 3 is the one most likely to be violated in practice, since team totals were your least liquid, widest-spread markets. That connects straight back to Q1: if the thin markets are the ones breaking consistency with the liquid ones, that's much stronger evidence about efficiency than spread width alone.

Snapshots arrive in bursts of 33 — and there are exactly 33 markets in the file (11 TOTAL + 14 TEAMTOTAL + 7 F5 + 1 RFI). That's the tell. Let me confirm the mechanism.

Short answer: update = an incremental book event as it happened; snapshot = a full-book resync that restates current state, whether or not anything changed. Four pieces of evidence.

1. Snapshots arrive in synchronized bursts of exactly 33 — the number of markets in the file.

2026-08-05 17:35:38    33     <- session start, every market at once
2026-08-05 17:42:58     1
2026-08-05 17:42:59    15
2026-08-05 17:51:04    33
2026-08-05 17:57:44    33
2026-08-05 18:04:42    33

218 distinct burst times, most of them all-33. Real market activity never hits 33 unrelated strikes in the same second. This is the collector (re)subscribing and the exchange replying with the full book for every subscription. The split bursts (17:42:58 + 17:42:59) are just one burst straddling a second boundary.

2. A worked example — KXMLBTEAMTOTAL-...-PIT7, a quiet market:

Every single snapshot that restates an old exchange_ts is byte-identical to the previous book — zero information content. That's the defining behavior of a resync. Overall 51% of snapshot rows carry no book change at all, versus 11% of updates.

The update-with-repeated-timestamp rows are a different animal: 87% of those did change the book. Those are multiple deltas sharing one millisecond stamp — the exchange batching several price-level changes at the same instant, delivered as separate messages.

4. Practical consequence: dedupe on snapshots, and never use them for timing. For any event-time analysis, update rows are your real feed. Snapshots are for state recovery — useful to confirm your reconstructed book hasn't drifted, useless as observations of market activity. Counting them as events would inflate update rates in quiet markets, exactly where you can least afford it.
