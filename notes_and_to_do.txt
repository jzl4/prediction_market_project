- What does the edge of 0.05 (55% win rate) mean in a market where you can buy or sell at price p?
- Let's assume that the market moves up or down 50% of the time. This is a reasonable assumption because markets are generally a random walk in short intervals of time
- You can predict this direction correctly 55% of the time - this is the signal that you have that gives you a 5% edge over random coin toss
- And assume that you get out of your trade after making or lose an amount delta, something like 0.1 or 0.2
- So for example, if you buy at p = 0.3, and delta = 0.1, you exit when p hits either $0.4 (you were right and made $0.1) or p hits $0.2 (you were wrong and lost $0.1)

- THE SPREAD: assume the bid/ask spread is $0.01, and p is the MID price. So the
  best bid is (p - 0.005) and the best ask is (p + 0.005).
  * If I am ACTIVE (a taker), I buy at the ask and sell at the bid. I pay away
    half the spread on the way in and half on the way out => I LOSE $0.01 per
    round trip, on top of the fees.
  * If I am PASSIVE (a maker), my resting bid gets hit at (p - 0.005) and my
    resting offer gets lifted at (p + 0.005). I COLLECT half the spread on the
    way in and half on the way out => I MAKE $0.01 per round trip, and pay no
    fees at all, because making is free.
  * So the spread swings a full $0.02 per round trip between being a taker and
    being a maker. That is bigger than delta itself at realistic move sizes.

- So there are 4 possible scenarios.  Let's walk through the 4 scenarios.
  Shown for the ACTIVE / taker case; the passive case is the same but with
  +0.005 instead of -0.005 at each leg, and with both fee terms deleted.

- I buy the contract at p and my signal was right
- I bought at (p + 0.005), I exit by selling at (p + delta - 0.005)
- Net move captured is (delta - 0.01), not delta -- the spread ate a penny
- I paid (p+0.005)*(1-p-0.005)*0.07 fee on the way in, and
  (p+delta-0.005)*(1-p-delta+0.005)*0.07 fee on the way out
- Net P&L = (delta - 0.01) - fee_in - fee_out
- This happens 0.5 x 0.55 = 27.5% of the time.  Generally: 0.5 x (signal win rate)

- I sell the contract at p and my signal was right
- I sold at (p - 0.005), and I bought back at (p - delta + 0.005)
- Net move captured is (delta - 0.01)
- I paid (p-0.005)*(1-p+0.005)*0.07 on the way in, and
  (p-delta+0.005)*(1-p+delta-0.005)*0.07 on the way out
- Net P&L = (delta - 0.01) - fee_in - fee_out
- This happens 0.5 x 0.55 = 27.5% of the time.  Generally: 0.5 x (signal win rate)

- I buy the contract at p and my signal was wrong
- I bought at (p + 0.005), I exit by selling at (p - delta - 0.005)
- Net loss is (delta + 0.01) -- the spread makes the loss WORSE, not better
- I paid (p+0.005)*(1-p-0.005)*0.07 on the way in, and
  (p-delta-0.005)*(1-p+delta+0.005)*0.07 on the way out
- Net P&L = -(delta + 0.01) - fee_in - fee_out
- This happens 0.5 x 0.45 = 22.5% of the time.  Generally: 0.5 x (1 - signal win rate)

- I sell the contract at p and my signal was wrong
- I sold at (p - 0.005), and I buy it back at (p + delta + 0.005)
- Net loss is (delta + 0.01)
- I paid (p-0.005)*(1-p+0.005)*0.07 on the way in, and
  (p+delta+0.005)*(1-p-delta-0.005)*0.07 on the way out
- Net P&L = -(delta + 0.01) - fee_in - fee_out
- This happens 0.5 x 0.45 = 22.5% of the time.    Generally: 0.5 x (1 - signal win rate)

One more caveat: assume that (p + delta) is capped at 1.0.  For example, if p = 0.95, and delta = 0.1, we can't actually trade at 1.05 because max is 1.0.  Similarly, assume that (p - delta) is floor at 0.0 (it cannot be below 0.0)

####################### Simulation of 4B #########################

THE CORE FORMULA: expected profit per contract = (w - p)
  Buy at price p. Right -> you get $1, so you made (1 - p). Wrong -> you made -p.
  EV = w*(1-p) - (1-w)*p = w - p.  Everything cancels.
  So a 55% win rate is NOT an edge by itself -- it is an edge relative to price:
      buy at 0.50 with w = 0.55  ->  edge = +0.05   (this is the question's setup)
      buy at 0.80 with w = 0.55  ->  edge = -0.25   (catastrophic)
  "50% breakeven" in the prompt only makes sense at p = 0.50, so the question is
  implicitly telling us to assume even-money trades in the base case.

--- THE PLAN, 4 STEPS ---

STEP 1 -- NAIVE SIM (constant size, p = 0.50)
  Win $X or lose $X, 55/45, 32,000 trades (500/day x 64 days). Measure P(cumulative
  P&L ever touches -$1,000) for several bet sizes. This is the base case the prompt
  literally describes.

STEP 2 -- REAL PRICE PATHS
  TRAP TO AVOID: do NOT hold w fixed at 0.55 while feeding in real prices. By
  EV = w - p that would make the edge +19c where a market traded at 0.36 and -41c
  where it traded at 0.96. Neither is a signal; the sim would just be measuring the
  average price of whichever market got picked, and the "answer" would be an
  artifact of that choice.

  FIX: the win rate has to move with the price. A signal does not give "55%" in the
  abstract -- it says a contract is MISPRICED BY SOME AMOUNT. So set
      true probability = p + 0.05      (market is 5 cents too cheap)
      we buy at p, so the edge is 5 cents everywhere
  At p = 0.50 this gives w = 0.55 and recovers the prompt exactly. Cap w at 1.0.

  WHAT THIS ACTUALLY SHOWS (the real point of step 2): with the fix, expected profit
  per trade is IDENTICAL across all 33 markets -- always 5 cents. So step 2 does not
  change the average outcome at all. What it changes is RISK OF RUIN:
      contract at 0.96: right most of the time, collect 4c; wrong -> lose 96c.
                        rare, brutal losses.
      contract at 0.10: bleed 10c most of the time; occasionally collect 90c.
                        frequent small losses, lumpy recovery.
  Same expected profit, very different odds of touching -$1,000 on the way.
  => WITH A FIXED LOSS BUDGET, WHERE ON THE PRICE LADDER YOU TRADE MATTERS AS MUCH
     AS THE SIZE OF YOUR EDGE. A naive random walk cannot show this. This is the
     differentiating insight for Q4b.

  SIZE IN DOLLARS AT RISK, NOT CONTRACTS. 100 contracts at 0.96 risks $96; 100 at
  0.10 risks $10. Not comparable. Since the constraint is a $1,000 budget, make
  dollars-at-risk the control variable: risk $X per trade -> buy X/p contracts.
  A loss is then always exactly $X and a win is X*(1-p)/p.

  CAVEAT TO STATE: the prompt says 500 UNCORRELATED trades per day. All 500 taken
  from one game's markets would be the opposite of uncorrelated -- every contract
  resolves off the same nine innings. Using a real price path is a device for
  getting realistic prices, not a claim of independence. In reality the 500 would
  be spread across many games.

STEP 3 -- DYNAMIC SIZING (resize every trade, not once a day)
  Resizing only at day boundaries is how you finish a day at -$5k after being down
  $900 at trade 274. Size must respond at every trade.
  WRINKLE: if sizing is proportional (always risk 10% of what is left), the budget
  mathematically CANNOT reach zero -- each loss removes a fraction of the remainder.
  P(ruin) then comes out 0 by construction and the metric becomes meaningless.
  So pick a different failure definition before coding: budget drops below a floor
  (e.g. $100, too small to trade), or just report the distribution of final P&L and
  the worst drawdown along the path.
  NOTE: because size depends on P&L and not on the date, the calendar stays
  irrelevant -- 32,000 trades is 32,000 trades whether it is 500/day for 64 days or
  250/day for 128.

STEP 4 -- ADD FEES
  Subtract p*(1-p)*0.07 per contract when taking. At p = 0.50 that is 1.75c against
  a 5c edge -- 35% OF THE EDGE GONE. Does not change the structure of the sim, one
  subtraction per trade, but it moves the answer a lot.

--- UNIT TEST FOR STEP 1 (do not derive, just check against it) ---
Gambler's ruin: for a +/-$X bet at a 55% win rate with a $1,000 floor, the chance of
ever hitting the floor is about (0.45/0.55) ^ (1000/X):
      $10  ->  essentially zero
      $50  ->  ~2%
      $100 ->  ~14%
      $200 ->  ~37%
Run the Monte Carlo at those four sizes. Land near these numbers -> logic is right.
Miss them -> there is a bug.

--- AGGREGATION: time-weight, do not take a median across rows ---
Book rows are events, not clock ticks. Message counts differ 12x across markets,
and inside one market the busy minutes generate far more rows than quiet ones.
A median across rows answers "what was the spread on a typical MESSAGE", which
oversamples exactly the active periods. We want "what was the spread on a typical
SECOND". Weight each quote by how long it stood before the next book row replaced
it: groupby.diff().shift(-1) gives the dwell time.

Checked the dwell-weighting trap: largest gap in the books feed is 1.0 minute out
of a 5.99-hour window (0.3%). Safe, no special handling needed.

--- CONTRACTS vs DOLLAR NOTIONAL (the long back-and-forth) ---
Both are right, they answer different questions. Report both columns.

Why contracts is the payoff-relevant unit for Q1:
every contract settles at exactly $0 or $1, so face value is IDENTICAL across all
33 markets. Quantity is already the notional-equivalent -- 10,000 contracts is
10,000 dollars of settlement exposure whether the market trades at 0.09 or 0.96.
This is the OPPOSITE of stocks, where you multiply by price precisely because face
values differ. Payoff scales with CONTRACTS, not capital: an edge of delta earns
delta dollars per contract regardless of p.

Why capital committed still matters -- same 3-cent edge, both books 10,000 deep:

    market at p = 0.09          market at p = 0.50
    think fair value is 0.12    think fair value is 0.53
    buy 10,000 contracts:
      cost           $900         cost           $5,000
      EV at settle $1,200         EV at settle   $5,300
      PROFIT         $300         PROFIT           $300   <- identical
      ret on capital  33%         ret on capital      6%
      max loss       $900         max loss       $5,000

Same expected profit, 5.5x the capital, 5.5x the max loss, one fifth the return on
capital. Contracts alone hides all of that. Max loss also feeds straight into Q4's
$1,000 loss tolerance.

If computing capital committed, it is SIDE-AWARE, not just qty * p:
    resting BID of Q at price p ties up  Q * p        (what the buyer pays)
    resting ASK of Q at price p ties up  Q * (1 - p)  (seller collects p, owes $1
                                                       if it settles yes)
Summing all 10 levels as qty * p would score the ask side of a 0.09 market at 9%
of its real capital at risk instead of 91%.

books in contracts but tiny capital committed is one where makers are risking very
little to look liquid -- that is evidence about how seriously to take that depth.
Better Q1 observation than either column alone.

Q1:
Market efficiency can be defined in several ways:
- Bid/ask is the narrowest, liquidity is the highest (volume traded is high)
- New information gets baked into the prices immediately.  In other words, a market who moves in 1 second after new information is more efficient than a market which takes 2 days to move and bake in (IPO market is much slower than HFT, for instance)
- Market efficiency can also be relative to other related markets in terms of lack of arbitrage opportunities. If the quoted markets are all CDFs (or survival functions which evaluate the probability of number of runs being greater than a value k), then certain qualities must hold, like the marginal probabilities (number of runs being exactly equal to a value k) must be strictly positive (net of transaction costs). Other qualities must hold as well, like the number of runs in first 5 innings must be less than, or equal to, the number of runs in the whole game (9 innings).  A market can still be efficient from lack of arbitrage opportunities, but if it is close to violating these conditions, like probability of k runs in first 5 innings is almost equal to probability of k runs in 9 innings, or if P(number of runs = exactly k) (which is a diff of two CDF markets), this is almost not efficient

--- Q4b: the Monte Carlo plan ---

Simulation is the right call. Three things determine whether it tells you
anything true.

1. GET THE PER-TRADE PAYOFF RIGHT. These are binary contracts, not symmetric
   bets. You pay p and receive 1 or 0, so a win is +(1-p) and a loss is -p. At
   p ~ 0.50 that's roughly symmetric, which the question's framing implies, but
   don't hardcode +/- $50 -- hardcode the contract.

2. THE FEE IS THE WHOLE STORY. At p = 0.50 the taker fee is 0.5*0.5*0.07 =
   0.0175. The edge is 5c (55% true vs 50c price). So:

       take:  EV = 0.55 - 0.5175 = +0.0325 per contract
       make:  EV = 0.55 - 0.5000 = +0.0500 per contract

   Crossing the spread eats 35% OF THE EDGE. Whether the signal can rest or must
   take is probably the biggest lever in the whole answer -- bigger than sizing.
   Simulate both.

3. DON'T FIX p AT 0.55. This is the one that matters. If you simulate assuming
   the edge is real, the sim will tell you the edge is real. With 500 uncorrelated
   trades/day a genuine 3.25% net edge is enormous -- daily Sharpe ~1.4, and over
   45,000 trades the terminal outcome is nearly deterministic. You'd conclude
   "size up, never bust", which is correct CONDITIONAL ON 55% BEING TRUE, and that
   condition is doing all the work.

   The real risk in 4b isn't variance, it's BEING WRONG ABOUT p. So sweep it: run
   the same sim at true p = 0.55, 0.52, 0.50, 0.48 and see what the $1,000
   tolerance implies in each. That turns paper-trade-vs-deploy into a real
   question instead of a rhetorical one.

   And it gives the sharp answer: the standard error of the win rate after N
   trades is sqrt(p(1-p)/N), so 500 trades -- ONE SINGLE DAY -- pins p to +/-2.2%.
   You can buy nearly all the information paper trading would give you by trading
   one day at minimum size, while keeping the P&L. The simulation demonstrates
   this rather than asserting it.

Two smaller notes:
- The closed form you're declining to derive is KELLY: f* = 2p - 1 = 10% of
  bankroll at even money. The dynamic sizing you described (scale up when ahead,
  down when behind) IS proportional Kelly betting. Worth naming in the write-up
  even though you got there by simulation -- arriving at it empirically is a fine
  answer.
- The binding constraint is MAX DRAWDOWN ALONG THE PATH, not terminal P&L. Have
  the sim track the running minimum, not just the final number.

WHY BASEBALL BREAKS POISSON: a Poisson process needs independent increments - each scoring
opportunity independent of the last. Baseball violates this structurally. Runs don't arrive
one at a time at a constant rate; they arrive in INNINGS, IN BURSTS. A rally puts runners on
base, which raises the probability of the next run, and a home run with two men on scores
three at once. Three outs then shut the inning down entirely. So you get many innings with
zero and occasional innings with four - clustering, which is exactly overdispersion. The
standard formalization is a Poisson-gamma mixture (lambda itself random, varying by matchup,
weather, bullpen), and that mixture IS the negative binomial.

averages 0.84/0.395 ~ 2.1 runs. That's on the high side. Either it reflects a genuine read on
this matchup - two strong starting pitchers making a scoreless first likely, with scoring
concentrated when it does break through - or it's a pricing quirk.

Flagging rather than concluding: no league baseline in hand to compare against. TO DO: look
up the MLB league-average P(at least 1 run in the 1st inning) and compare.

But remember RFI is the market with the anomalous 428k top-of-book depth and the most trades
in the whole dataset. If we go looking for the "most informed action" in Q3, a first-inning
market that is both the most heavily traded AND possibly priced away from a naive baseline is
a natural place to start.


###################################


So the constraint set for the no-arb check is:

1. Within a chain — S(n) monotonically decreasing, and every differenced bucket ≥ 0 (call spreads non-negative, butterflies non-negative).
2. Across nested chains — RFI ⊆ F5 ⊆ game total, so at any shared strike, S_RFI(n) ≤ S_F5(n) ≤ S_total(n).
3. Teams to total — the convolution of the two team distributions should reproduce the game-total distribution; the weak but always-valid version is S_total(n) ≥ max(S_MIL(n), S_PIT(n)).
4. Means add exactly — E[MIL] + E[PIT] = E[total].

Constraint 3 is the one most likely to be violated in practice, since team totals were your least liquid, widest-spread markets. That connects straight back to Q1: if the thin markets are the ones breaking consistency with the liquid ones, that's much stronger evidence about efficiency than spread width alone.

Me: What do you think the 2 types of msg_type "update" and "snapshot" represent in df_books?  Justify your answer with examples

Snapshots arrive in bursts of 33 — and there are exactly 33 markets in the file (11 TOTAL + 14 TEAMTOTAL + 7 F5 + 1 RFI). That's the tell. Let me confirm the mechanism.

Short answer: update = an incremental book event as it happened; snapshot = a full-book resync that restates current state, whether or not anything changed. Four pieces of evidence.

1. Snapshots arrive in synchronized bursts of exactly 33 — the number of markets in the file.

2026-08-05 17:35:38    33     <- session start, every market at once
2026-08-05 17:42:58     1
2026-08-05 17:42:59    15
2026-08-05 17:51:04    33
2026-08-05 17:57:44    33
2026-08-05 18:04:42    33

218 distinct burst times, most of them all-33. Real market activity never hits 33 unrelated strikes in the same second. This is the collector (re)subscribing and the exchange replying with the full book for every subscription. The split bursts (17:42:58 + 17:42:59) are just one burst straddling a second boundary.

2. A worked example — KXMLBTEAMTOTAL-...-PIT7, a quiet market:

recv          exchange      type       apparent lat   book
17:35:38.77   17:33:53.62   snapshot       105.15s    0.14/0.16  338/3225
17:36:03.67   17:36:03.65   update           0.02s    0.14/0.16  288/3225
17:41:00.86   17:41:00.83   update           0.02s    0.14/0.16  288/3225
17:45:57.83   17:45:57.81   update           0.02s    0.14/0.16  288/3225
17:49:01.98   17:49:01.32   update           0.66s    0.14/0.16  288/3225
17:51:04.52   17:49:01.32   snapshot       123.20s    0.14/0.16  288/3225
17:57:44.77   17:49:01.32   snapshot       523.45s    0.14/0.16  288/3225

Look at the last two rows. Both snapshots carry exchange_ts = 17:49:01.32 — the timestamp of the last time the book actually changed. Nothing happened in this market for eight minutes, so each resync keeps restating that stale timestamp. Updates, by contrast, are stamped when the event occurred and land ~20ms later.

3. The content check confirms it. Splitting rows by whether exchange_ts repeats the previous message in that market:

┌──────────┬──────────────────────────┬────────┬────────────────────────┐
│ msg_type │ repeats prev exchange_ts │   n    │ book identical to prev │
├──────────┼──────────────────────────┼────────┼────────────────────────┤
│ snapshot │ yes                      │ 2,198  │ 100%                   │
├──────────┼──────────────────────────┼────────┼────────────────────────┤

│ update   │ no                       │ 49,111 │ 11%                    │
└──────────┴──────────────────────────┴────────┴────────────────────────┘

Every single snapshot that restates an old exchange_ts is byte-identical to the previous book — zero information content. That's the defining behavior of a resync. Overall 51% of snapshot rows carry no book change at all, versus 11% of updates.

The update-with-repeated-timestamp rows are a different animal: 87% of those did change the book. Those are multiple deltas sharing one millisecond stamp — the exchange batching several price-level changes at the same instant, delivered as separate messages.

4. Practical consequence: dedupe on snapshots, and never use them for timing. For any event-time analysis, update rows are your real feed. Snapshots are for state recovery — useful to confirm your reconstructed book hasn't drifted, useless as observations of market activity. Counting them as events would inflate update rates in quiet markets, exactly where you can least afford it.

This partly rewrites your latency note. Dropping snapshots and repeated-timestamp rows:

clean (49,111 updates):   median 0.022s   p90 34.2s   p99  42.8s   max  385.6s

The scary tail was mostly an artifact — max 1943s was a snapshot restating a book that hadn't moved in 32 minutes, not a half-hour collector stall. Same for the 24-minute gap you saw between the books' earliest exchange_ts (17:11:13) and earliest recv_ts (17:35:34): that's the opening snapshot burst reporting a book last touched at 17:11.
