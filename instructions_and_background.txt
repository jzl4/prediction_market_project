# Background about the data & sports betting markets
- The vendor is River Markets, which is a prime broker / unified execution layer for prediction markets - one API across venues, live on Kalshi, Polymarket, Polymarket.  They raised $8.5 million seed announced on Aug 11th, 2026
- The ID native_id, such as KXMLBTOTAL-26AUG051940PITMIL-9, is one specific bet, such as "Will the Fed hike in September" or "Will the Giants win the Superbowl?".  One question, one order book, one price.  Prices are between 0 and 1, and correspond to an implied probability of the event happening. For instance, a price of 0.47 means the market believes there is a 47% chance that Fed will hike in Sept. If someone buys at 0.47, and the Fed does hike, he receives $1 and makes a profit of $0.43
- Distinction between native_id vs. river_id: river_id is River's venue-neutral number for the same thing, so their customers can manage one set of identifiers, instead of dealing with both Kalshi's identifiers and Polymarket's condition-ID identifiers. The reason why they're 1-to-1 here is because this file quotes markets/bets from only one venue.  If instead, the bet/prediction traded on both Kalshi and Polymarket, we'd get 2 different river_ids.  For the purposes of this exercise, just treat native_id and river_id as the same

# Baseball
- The prediction markets in this dataset relate to baseball, specifically concerning the number of runs (a player crossing home plate)
- Example: P(runs >= 8) = 0.445 means 44.5% chance of there being 8 or more runs in this game
- Therefore, the strike k where the probability crosses 0.5 (50% chance) is where the market expects the median number of runs to happen in a game. It's around k = 7 to 8 runs, because P(runs >= 7) and P(runs >= 8) are the closest values to 50%. Specifically, this happens at strike 7 with probability 0.565 and strike 8 with probability 0.445.  But this is the median, not the average/mean.  The mean is higher because the run distribution is necessarily bounded at zero on the left side and unbounded on the right side (number of runs must be 0 or greater, but there is no upper limit), so mean > median due to right-skew
- The reason why the strikes are at half integer increments like 1.5, is because the event can never expire on that strike.  It must expire at 1, 2, 3, etc, so it is unambiguous whether or not the contract pays out or not.  For instance, contract KXMLBTOTAL-9 means 9 or more runs, which is identical to "over 8.5".  In other words, strike of 1.5 means P(number or runs is 2 or more).  So P(number of runs = exactly 2) = P(runs >= 2) - P(runs >= 3)
- team_totals is one team on its own; totals is both teams combined. The data:

line                       1.5    2.5    3.5    4.5    5.5    6.5    7.5
Milwaukee Brewers;Over    0.805  0.650  0.495  0.355  0.235  0.155  0.095
Pittsburgh Pirates;Over   0.770  0.605  0.450  0.315  0.210  0.135  0.085

game TOTAL at line 3.5  = 0.845

At the same 3.5 strike, each individual team is near 0.45-0.50 but the combined game total is 0.845 — because getting 4+ runs is far easier for two teams together than for either alone. Each team's median is ~3.5 runs; the two sum to ~7, which lines up with the game total's median of 7-8. That's your additivity constraint holding.

That's also why there are 14 team-total markets: 2 teams × 7 strikes. And it's visible in outcome_name, which reads "Milwaukee Brewers;Over" vs "Pittsburgh Pirates;Over" — the team is named because the contract is about that team alone. Incidentally, Milwaukee prices above Pittsburgh at every strike, so the market expects Milwaukee to score more.

Every contract in this file is cumulative — P(runs ≥ n). That's a survival function, and it is monotonically decreasing by definition. That's your call-spread argument and it always holds:

P(>= 2) = 0.965
P(>= 3) = 0.935
P(>= 4) = 0.845   ... strictly decreasing all the way down to

P(>=12) = 0.145

Note: This is actually 1 - CDF from statistics, because in statistics, we quote P(X < some constant k), which is the other way around.  In sports betting on Kalshi, this is the survival function, which is the complement of CDF.  Survival function must be monotonically decreasing, just like how the CDF is monotonically increasing towards 1.0

But P(exactly n) is what you get by differencing adjacent strikes, and that is emphatically not monotone — it's a hump:

P(exactly  1) = 0.035  ####
P(exactly  2) = 0.030  ###
P(exactly  3) = 0.090  ##########
P(exactly  5) = 0.120  ##############
P(exactly  7) = 0.120  ##############
P(exactly  9) = 0.100  ###########
P(exactly 11) = 0.070  ########

P(exactly 7 runs) is nearly four times P(exactly 1 run). Runs are roughly Poisson-distributed with a mean near 8, so the density peaks in the middle and falls off both sides.

Note: This is basically the PMF (probability mass function from university statistics), and it is the derivative of the survival or CDF function
